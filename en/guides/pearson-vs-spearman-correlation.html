Pearson vs Spearman correlation: which one to use

Updated October 1, 2026 · 10 min read · By tcctools Editorial Team

Measuring association between continuous or ordinal variables is a cornerstone of scientific inquiry. However, many authors default to Pearson's r without verifying bivariate normality or checking whether the relationship is truly linear. Violating these assumptions can lead to severely biased estimates or harsh review critiques.

2. Linear vs. monotonic relationships

Understanding the distinction is vital:

  • Linear: Points lie along a straight line; the expected change in Y per unit increase in X remains constant across the entire distribution.
  • Monotonic: As X increases, Y consistently increases (or consistently decreases), but not necessarily at a constant rate. Spearman evaluates the monotonicity of raw values by assessing the linearity of their ranked values.

3. Survey questions and Likert scales

Single survey items scored on a Likert scale (e.g., 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) are ordinal data. Distances between successive numbers cannot be assumed equal. Under strict statistical methodology, Spearman rank correlation is the correct choice. Only when multiple Likert items are summed or averaged into a validated continuous composite scale can Pearson correlation be considered.

4. APA 7th Edition reporting templates

Reporting Pearson's r:

"A Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient was computed to assess the relationship between study hours and exam scores. There was a moderate positive correlation, r(82) = .54, 95% CI [.37, .68], p < .001."

Reporting Spearman's rs:

"A Spearman rank-order correlation revealed a statistically significant negative association between job satisfaction rankings and turnover intent, rs(118) = −.42, p < .001."
